Mecca pact officials in Tehran in bid to defuse Iran-US and Yemen tensions

Saudi, Pakistani and Turkish officials met Iran's president as fears grow of new US strikes and Houthi attacks on Saudi Arabia continue.

26 October 2026, 23:18 · Source: Al Jazeera

With fear rising across the Middle East that the U.S. and Israel will restart military strikes on Iran, diplomats from Saudi Arabia, Pakistan and Turkey met with Iranian leaders in Tehran in a new push to negotiate an end to the war. https://t.co/0aEnNkkZfk

Senior officials from Saudi Arabia, Pakistan and Turkey, the three countries behind the Mecca Joint Defence Agreement, arrived in Tehran on Monday for talks on regional security as the threat of fresh US strikes on Iran hangs over the region.

Saudi Defence Minister Prince Khalid bin Salman, Pakistan's army chief Asim Munir and Turkish intelligence chief Ibrahim Kalin met Iranian President Masoud Pezeshkian. They are also due to hold discussions with Mohsen Rezaei, the head of Iran's security body. Little has been said publicly about the agenda, but the visit is among the highest-level exchanges held in Tehran in recent months.

A central issue is expected to be the missile and drone attacks launched by Yemen's Houthi rebels, who are allied with Tehran, against Saudi Arabia. Riyadh backs Yemen's internationally recognised government. Analysts quoted by Al Jazeera said Pakistan and Turkey are unlikely to send troops to Yemen despite the collective defence arrangement, even as Saudi expectations of its partners grow.

The trip follows a visit to Saudi Arabia and Iran by Munir last week. It also comes as Pakistan restricts overland trade with Iran, amid the US naval blockade of Iranian ports. Iranian officials say they favour security that comes from within the region.

The backdrop is a standoff between Washington and Tehran. Tehran wants the US blockade of its southern ports lifted, which has halted its oil exports, and a return to a memorandum of understanding agreed in June. The US has refused. It wants shipping through the Strait of Hormuz to flow freely, tighter limits on Iran's nuclear programme and international inspections of its highly enriched uranium. President Donald Trump is reported to be weighing new strikes, and has described the choice as one between further diplomacy and a major attack on Iranian civilian infrastructure. Israel has said it wants the Islamic Republic overthrown.

Analysts were divided on what the visit can achieve. Ali Vaez of the International Crisis Group described it as a last-minute attempt to strengthen those in Tehran who favour compromise, but warned that goodwill alone would not be enough without a workable plan to bridge the gap between Washington and Tehran. Umer Karim, who studies Saudi foreign policy, said little has changed in Iran's strategic thinking and the visitors lack the leverage to alter it, so the talks are likely to focus on protecting Saudi Arabia if fighting resumes.

The US State Department ordered non-emergency government staff and their families to leave Saudi Arabia on 21 October, citing the Houthi threat.
