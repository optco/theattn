The “Cancer-Killing Pill” AOH1996: Hype, Hope and the Hard Truth

A twice-daily pill that wiped out more than 70 types of cancer cells in the lab is now being tested in patients with solid tumours and leukaemia. Here is what is proven, and what is not.

16 November 2026, 10:09 · Source: City of Hope, ClinicalTrials.gov, Cell Chemical Biology

Few drug names have travelled the internet as fast as AOH1996. Since researchers at City of Hope, the US cancer centre in California, first described it as a “cancer-killing pill”, headlines have claimed it could “annihilate” solid tumours. More than three years on, the drug is still very much in testing, and the real story is more careful, and in some ways more interesting.

What is AOH1996?

AOH1996 is an experimental oral drug taken as a pill. It belongs to a new class of medicines called PCNA inhibitors. PCNA, short for proliferating cell nuclear antigen, is a protein that helps cells copy and repair their DNA. Scientists long considered it “undruggable”, meaning no one had found a way to target it safely.

The City of Hope team, led by Professor Linda Malkas, who spent around two decades on the project, designed AOH1996 to go after a form of PCNA that is altered in cancer cells while leaving the normal version in healthy cells alone. The drug was licensed to a biotech start-up co-founded by Malkas.

The snowstorm that grounds the flights

Malkas compared PCNA to a major airline hub. Her image: the pill is like a snowstorm that closes the hub, shutting down every flight carrying cancer cells while healthy traffic carries on.

In scientific terms, the drug interferes with what are called transcription-replication conflicts. These are collisions that happen inside dividing cells when the machinery that copies DNA runs into the machinery that reads genes. Cancer cells suffer far more of these collisions than healthy cells. AOH1996 makes the clash worse in cancer cells, causing DNA damage that the cells cannot repair, and they die. The mechanism was detailed in a study published in Cell Chemical Biology in August 2023.

What the lab results showed

In laboratory tests, AOH1996 killed cancer cells from more than 70 different cell lines, including breast, prostate, brain, ovarian, cervical, skin and lung cancers. In mice, it suppressed tumour growth in models of neuroblastoma, breast cancer and small-cell lung cancer. Tests in mice and dogs showed no signs of toxicity to healthy cells, even at doses six times higher than the effective dose. Further experiments suggested it can make cancer cells more vulnerable to chemotherapy drugs that work by damaging DNA.

A separate preclinical study published in Frontiers in Pharmacology in 2026 found that the drug reduced the growth and spread of non-small cell lung cancer cells, again without toxicity in the model used.

Where it stands in human trials

This is the crucial point. Everything above comes from cells and animals. In people, AOH1996 is still at phase 1, the earliest stage of human testing, which is designed to check safety, side effects and the best dose, not to prove that a drug cures anything.

  • Refractory solid tumours: A first-in-human phase 1 trial began at City of Hope in late 2022. Patients take the pill twice a day for 28-day cycles. The trial record was updated as recently as October 2026, and also includes testing the drug alongside a targeted therapy in non-small cell lung cancer.
  • Relapsed or refractory acute myeloid leukaemia (AML): A newer phase 1 study, last updated in September 2026, is testing AOH1996 in combination with the leukaemia drugs azacitidine and venetoclax.

No full phase 1 results showing how well the pill works in patients have been published yet. City of Hope has said only that its first patient, who had recurrent solid tumours, was responding well early on.

Why the name matters

The drug carries a deeply human story. AOH1996 is named after Anna Olivia Healy, who was born in 1996 and died of the childhood cancer neuroblastoma at the age of nine. Her memory has driven much of the work behind the drug.

The bottom line

AOH1996 is a promising idea, tackling a protein once thought impossible to hit, and the lab evidence is strong. But “kills cancer in the lab” is a long way from “cures cancer in patients”. Most drugs that look excellent in early studies fail at later stages, and any approval would need phase 2 and phase 3 trials, which take years. The pill is not available to the public outside clinical trials.

Patients and families should discuss any trial options with their oncologist, and be wary of anyone selling “AOH1996” online. It is an investigational drug, and it cannot legitimately be bought.
