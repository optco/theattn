Retatrutide explained: approval status, how it works, trial results and side effects

Retatrutide is an investigational weekly injection from Eli Lilly that targets three hormone receptors. It is not yet approved anywhere, and Lilly plans to apply to the FDA in early 2027.

29 October 2026, 21:30 · Source: Drugs.com, HCPLive, Eli Lilly

Retatrutide is an investigational once-weekly injectable peptide developed by Eli Lilly and Company. It is a "triple agonist" that activates three hormone receptors: GLP-1, GIP and glucagon.

Current approval status

  • Not approved: Retatrutide is not approved by the US Food and Drug Administration (FDA) or any other global regulatory authority.
  • Timeline: Eli Lilly plans to submit a Biologics License Application (BLA) to the FDA for obesity and related conditions in early 2027, with type 2 diabetes submissions to follow.
  • Availability: It is currently restricted to clinical trials and cannot be legally prescribed or purchased. Unregulated products sold online or in spas as "retatrutide" are unverified, illegal and potentially dangerous.

How it works

  • Triple receptor action: Unlike single-receptor drugs (GLP-1, such as Ozempic) or dual-receptor drugs (GLP-1/GIP, such as Zepbound), retatrutide adds glucagon receptor activity.
  • Metabolic impact: This combination slows digestion, suppresses appetite and also increases energy expenditure and fat burning.

Clinical trial results

  • Weight loss: Phase 3 trials have shown average body weight reductions ranging from roughly 17% to about 28%, depending on dose and trial duration, outperforming current market standards such as semaglutide.
  • Type 2 diabetes: In head-to-head data, such as the TRANSCEND-T2D-2 trial, retatrutide produced substantial reductions in HbA1c and weight compared with 2 mg Ozempic.
  • Other conditions studied: Researchers are also evaluating it for moderate-to-severe obstructive sleep apnea, knee osteoarthritis pain and metabolic dysfunction-associated steatotic liver disease (MASLD).

Side effects

  • Gastrointestinal issues: The most common adverse events are mild-to-moderate nausea, diarrhea, vomiting and constipation.
  • Other effects: Clinical data also note possible temporary increases in heart rate, fatigue and unusual skin sensations such as hyperesthesia or tingling.
