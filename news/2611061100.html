Living drugs: the promise and pitfalls of CAR T-cell therapy

CAR T-cell therapy turns a patient's own immune cells into a precision weapon against cancer, but toxicity, cost and manufacturing delays still limit who can benefit.

6 November 2026, 11:00 · Source: Science & Health Desk

In modern oncology, few developments have inspired as much hope, or raised as many questions about ethics and logistics, as chimeric antigen receptor (CAR) T-cell therapy. Often called a "living drug," the treatment turns a patient's own immune system into a precision weapon against cancer.

The therapy has produced striking remission rates in conditions once considered incurable, but physicians and researchers caution that significant biological, financial and logistical barriers remain.

How it works

Unlike traditional chemotherapy, which floods the body with toxic compounds to kill rapidly dividing cells, CAR T-cell therapy is a form of cell-based gene engineering. It happens in four steps:

1. Extraction. T cells, the white blood cells that target threats, are collected from the patient's blood through a process called leukapheresis.
2. Reprogramming. In a specialised laboratory, scientists use a deactivated virus or gene-editing tools such as CRISPR to insert a gene into the T cells. The gene instructs them to produce synthetic receptors called chimeric antigen receptors (CARs).
3. Multiplication. The engineered cells, now equipped to home in on specific proteins on cancer cells, are grown in the millions.
4. Reinfusion. The patient receives a dose of their own upgraded immune cells, which hunt down and destroy the targeted cancer.

From terminal diagnoses to lasting remission

Several patients illustrate how the therapy moved from an experimental concept to standard of care. In 2012, six-year-old Emily Whitehead became the first paediatric patient to receive experimental CAR T-cell therapy after relapsing twice with B-cell acute lymphoblastic leukaemia. Despite severe acute side effects during treatment, she achieved complete remission and has remained cancer-free for more than a decade, becoming a global symbol of the therapy's curative potential.

Doug Olson and William Ludwig, both with advanced chronic lymphocytic leukaemia, were treated in 2010 in early-phase trials at the University of Pennsylvania. Follow-up studies published years later showed the engineered cells were still present in their blood long after their tumours had vanished.

Major limitations

CAR T-cell therapy is far from a universal cure. Oncologists face several serious obstacles in daily practice:

Severe toxicities. Cytokine release syndrome (CRS) and neurotoxicity known as ICANS can cause massive systemic inflammation, with high fevers, organ failure, seizures or swelling in the brain.
Solid tumour resistance. Physical barriers and an immunosuppressive tumour environment mean the therapy works well in blood cancers such as leukaemias and lymphomas, but has had far less success against solid tumours such as breast, lung and brain cancers.
Antigen escape. Cancer cells can mutate and stop displaying the target protein, hiding from the engineered T cells and leading to late-stage relapses.
Manufacturing delays. Because each dose is made from the patient's own cells, processing takes two to four weeks, and rapidly progressing patients may deteriorate while they wait.
Cost. Individualised cell manufacturing means treatment often costs more than US$400,000 to US$500,000 per dose, and total hospital care can push the bill past US$1 million.

The road ahead

To overcome these barriers, researchers are exploring allogeneic CAR T-cell therapy, which uses donor cells to create off-the-shelf treatments and removes the manufacturing delay. They are also developing dual-targeting CARs that bind to more than one cancer protein to prevent antigen escape. Trials are expanding beyond cancer into autoimmune diseases such as lupus and multiple sclerosis.

CAR T-cell therapy is one of the most remarkable breakthroughs in modern medicine. Turning it from an expensive, niche treatment into a globally accessible cure remains one of medicine's greatest challenges.
