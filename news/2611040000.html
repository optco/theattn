Psyllium husk cut LDL cholesterol by 13 points across 28 trials, and it doesn’t work the way you think

A review of 28 randomized trials found about 10 grams of psyllium a day lowered LDL by roughly 13 mg/dL. The secret is a gel in your gut that traps bile acids, not anything the body absorbs.

4 November 2026, 00:00 · Source: American Journal of Clinical Nutrition

Psyllium, the seed husk of Plantago ovata sold as a fiber supplement, lowers LDL cholesterol by about 13 mg/dL, according to a 2018 analysis of 28 randomized controlled trials published in the American Journal of Clinical Nutrition. The way it works is often described wrongly: it is not absorbed by the body, and it does not scrub cholesterol out of the gut. It works mechanically.

How it works

When psyllium reaches the small intestine and takes on water, it forms a thick, viscous gel. That gel traps bile acids, the molecules the liver makes from cholesterol to help digest fat. Normally most bile acids are reabsorbed and recycled back to the liver. When psyllium holds them back, the liver has to make replacements, and it draws on cholesterol to do so. It pulls more LDL out of the blood to refill its supply, and blood LDL falls. Prescription bile acid sequestrants such as cholestyramine are used for the same purpose.

Because psyllium is not absorbed or broken down, it does not enter the bloodstream or act on a receptor, which helps explain its mild side-effect profile.

What the trials found

The analysis, led by Elena Jovanovski and colleagues, pooled 28 trials with 1,924 participants, lasting at least three weeks, in people with and without high cholesterol. At a median dose of about 10.2 grams a day, LDL fell by 0.33 mmol/L, which is about 13 mg/dL. Non-HDL cholesterol fell by 0.39 mmol/L, about 15 mg/dL. ApoB, a count of cholesterol-carrying particles that some experts consider a better predictor of heart disease than LDL, fell by 0.05 g/L.

The authors rated the evidence for apoB as high quality. The evidence for LDL and non-HDL was rated moderate because the trials were not fully consistent with each other.

Not all “soluble fiber” is the same

The key property is viscosity. Fibers that dissolve in water but do not form a thick gel, and that gut bacteria ferment quickly, such as inulin, should not be expected to work the same way, even though both are labelled soluble fiber.

How big is the effect?

A drop of 13 mg/dL is meaningful but modest. Statins generally lower LDL by considerably more, so psyllium is not a replacement for a statin when one has been prescribed. For people with borderline numbers who want to try diet-based options first, it is one of the few with this level of trial evidence.

Practical points

The trials’ median dose was about 10 grams a day, and many split it into two or three doses with meals. It should be taken with a full glass of water, and people new to it can start with about 5 grams and increase slowly to limit bloating and gas. Anyone who takes regular medication, or has swallowing or bowel problems, should check with a doctor or pharmacist first.
