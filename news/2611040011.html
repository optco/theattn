China finds a real-life “Three-Body” star system: a dead star spinning 300 times a second

Astronomers using the FAST telescope found a pulsar locked in a stable dance with a white dwarf and a Sun-like star, and the third star explains why the pulsar is slowing down so oddly.

4 November 2026, 00:11 · Source: CGTN

A dead star spinning more than 300 times a second is locked in a cosmic dance with two other stars, and Chinese astronomers say it is only the second confirmed triple star system known to contain a pulsar, not counting pulsars with planets.

The team from the National Astronomical Observatories of the Chinese Academy of Sciences, led by Han Jinlin, used FAST, the world’s largest single-dish radio telescope, to study a pulsar called PSR J0435+3233. The findings were published in The Astrophysical Journal Letters on 9 October 2026, alongside work by a European team.

Three stars, three life stages

A pulsar is the collapsed core of a dead star that sweeps a beam of radio waves past Earth with every turn. This one spins once every 3.2 milliseconds, more than 300 times a second. It circles a white dwarf, the dense core of another dead star, once every eight days. That pair is in turn orbited by a Sun-like star on a stretched-out path that takes about 73.5 years.

The researchers believe the three stars were born together. The heaviest exploded and became the pulsar, the second became a white dwarf, and the lightest is still evolving. One day it too should become a white dwarf, leaving a stable system of one pulsar and two white dwarfs.

Not like the novel

Unlike the chaotic three-sun world of Liu Cixin’s novel The Three-Body Problem, this system is stable. The distant third star also solves a puzzle: the pulsar’s spin is slowing down about 100 times faster than in similar pulsars, and the pull of the third star explains why.

A laboratory for gravity

Pulsars tick like extremely precise clocks, so systems like this one are natural laboratories for testing Einstein’s theory of gravity, including the strong equivalence principle. The only other known pulsar triple, PSR J0337+1715, was found with the Green Bank Telescope in the United States and has a pulsar and two white dwarfs.

The pulsar was first detected with FAST on 8 June 2020. The researchers combined five years of radio observations with public infrared, optical and gamma-ray data to identify the third star.
