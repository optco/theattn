Frog Gut Bacteria Eliminates Colorectal Tumors in Mice With 100% Success Rate

Researchers at the Japan Advanced Institute of Science and Technology successfully eradicated colorectal tumors in preclinical models using a single injection of bacteria isolated from Japanese tree frogs.

28 October 2026, 10:00 · Source: TheAttn Science Desk

Researchers led by Professor Eijiro Miyako at the Japan Advanced Institute of Science and Technology (JAIST) have successfully eliminated colorectal tumors in mice using a single injection of bacteria isolated from the gut of Japanese tree frogs.

Published in the journal Gut Microbes, the study focused on the bacterium Ewingella americana, which demonstrated a 100 percent complete response rate in preclinical mouse models.

The therapy relies on a dual action mechanism that takes advantage of the unique microenvironment found inside solid tumors. Ewingella americana is a facultative anaerobe, meaning it thrives in low oxygen environments. Once injected intravenously, the bacteria navigate leaky tumor blood vessels and selectively multiply inside the hypoxic core of the tumor. The bacteria increase roughly 3,000 fold within 24 hours, destroying cancer cells directly.

Simultaneously, the presence of the bacteria triggers a potent localized immune response. It recruits immune cells, including T cells, B cells, and neutrophils, to the tumor site. These cells release pro inflammatory cytokines, such as TNF alpha and IFN gamma, which further accelerate cancer cell death.

The safety profile observed in the study was highly favorable. The bacteria cleared from the bloodstream within approximately 24 hours and did not colonize vital organs such as the liver, kidneys, lungs, or heart. The mice experienced only mild, transient inflammation that resolved within 72 hours, with no evidence of chronic toxicity during a 60 day observation period.

In the study mouse model, the single dose bacterial injection outperformed standard chemotherapy regimens, such as liposomal doxorubicin, as well as immune checkpoint inhibitors like anti PD L1 antibodies.

However, critical context is required when interpreting these results. While these preclinical findings show significant promise for bacterial based cancer therapies, this treatment is currently limited to animal models. Human clinical trials have not yet begun.

It is a well documented blind spot in oncology research that murine models frequently show near perfect efficacy for treatments that fail entirely in human trials. Mouse immune systems and tumor microenvironments differ substantially from those of humans. A 100 percent response rate in mice is an encouraging starting point, but it is not a predictor of human clinical success.

Researchers are now expanding tests to evaluate the treatment across other solid tumor types, including breast, pancreatic, and skin cancers. They are also working to optimize delivery methods prior to any potential human trials.
