Which Chinese Car Brands Are Most Trusted, and Which Are Not

Geely and BYD earn the strongest reputations for quality, while MG sits near the bottom of Western reliability surveys. Here is how the top and bottom Chinese brands compare.

21 November 2026, 01:14 · Source: J.D. Power, Driver Power, What Car?

No single global body tracks every Chinese carmaker on every continent, so opinion depends on where you look. Hard reliability surveys such as J.D. Power in China and Driver Power and What Car? in the UK tell one story. Owner feedback from export markets such as Australia, South Africa, Europe, Latin America and the Middle East tells another.

Put together, Geely and BYD hold the strongest overall reputations, while Chery leads the domestic dependability studies.

The most praised: Geely and BYD

Geely earns the most credit for build quality, safety and mechanical engineering. It owns Volvo and shares platforms and safety standards with its European subsidiaries, so reviewers and buyers see Geely, along with Lynk & Co and Zeekr, as the closest Chinese match to European engineering.

BYD is praised most for hybrid and electric powertrain reliability and battery life. In the UK's 2026 Driver Power survey it reportedly placed 16th of 30 brands with a reliability score of 85.14%, ahead of several long-established Western names.

Top five for quality and reliability

1. Geely (including Zeekr and Lynk & Co). Praised for Volvo-derived safety design, solid chassis tuning and consistent five-star Euro NCAP results. It ranks among the top three domestic brands in J.D. Power's China Vehicle Dependability Study. Weak spots: overly aggressive driver-assistance tuning and occasional infotainment bugs in export markets.

2. BYD. Makes its own batteries, motors and chips, which keeps hardware failures low. Owner satisfaction is strong in Europe, Australia and Southeast Asia. Weak spots: paint and trim wear, and slow supply of collision parts outside China.

3. Chery (including Omoda and Jaecoo). Ranked first among Chinese domestic brands in J.D. Power's study, at 188 problems per 100 vehicles. Export buyers in harsh-climate markets such as South Africa and the Middle East like its durable engines and strong warranties. Weak spots: thirsty older turbo-petrol engines, clunky dual-clutch gearboxes in stop-start traffic, and growing pains from rapid expansion, including recalls.

4. Changan. Favours conservative, proven mechanicals and often pairs its engines with Japanese Aisin automatic gearboxes rather than fragile dry dual-clutch units. Scores well in Middle Eastern and domestic quality studies. Weak spot: a smaller dealer and parts network in several Western and Southern Hemisphere markets.

5. Great Wall Motor (Haval and Tank). Well regarded in Australia, South Africa and Russia for rugged body-on-frame SUVs and well-equipped crossovers. Weak spots: heavy fuel use, intrusive lane-keep assist and jerky low-speed tuning on hybrids.

Bottom five

The lower end includes high-volume exporters that draw regular owner complaints, as well as budget brands that hurt Chinese cars' image abroad.

1. SAIC / MG. Despite big sales in the UK and Australia, MG has repeatedly finished at or near the bottom of Western surveys. It reportedly came last (31st of 31) in What Car?'s reliability survey and 29th of 30 in the 2026 Driver Power survey. Owners report electrical faults, infotainment crashes, early suspension wear and rust or paint problems, mainly on budget petrol and early electric models.

2. BAIC. Relies on older licensed designs, with uneven quality control in export markets. Owners cite weak interior trim, sluggish gearboxes, poor resale value and thin parts support.

3. JAC Motors. Widely exported to Latin America, Africa and the Middle East as a budget pickup and crossover maker. It draws repeated criticism over rust protection, basic cabins, electrical faults and long-term durability.

4. DFSK / Dongfeng budget passenger models. Dongfeng builds solid commercial trucks and joint-venture cars, but its budget own-brand exports rank poorly in European and Latin American owner forums for weak crash performance, dated powertrains and component failures after about 50,000 km.

5. Brilliance and Landwind (legacy export tier). Both collapsed out of Western markets after scoring zero to one star in European crash tests and suffering serious mechanical and structural failures. They remain the benchmark for the worst of Chinese export quality, even as newer firms such as Leapmotor and XPeng draw criticism for software bugs and long waits for accident spare parts.

The blind spot in most rankings

Public opinion often confuses mechanical reliability with after-sales service. Across almost every Chinese brand, including BYD and Geely, engine, motor and battery failures are now relatively rare. The biggest source of owner anger worldwide is waiting 30 to 120 days for body panels and electronic modules after a minor accident, along with driver-assistance software that is poorly calibrated for non-Chinese roads.
