US aid to Israel since 1948 totals about $300 billion, adjusted for inflation

Israel is the largest cumulative recipient of US foreign aid since World War II. Here is what the numbers show, what the current agreement guarantees, and where supporters and critics disagree.

2 November 2026, 12:00 · Source: Congressional Research Service, Council on Foreign Relations

The United States has given Israel roughly $300 billion in economic, military and missile-defence assistance since 1948 when adjusted for inflation, making it the largest cumulative recipient of US foreign aid since World War II.

The Congressional Research Service (CRS), which advises US lawmakers, estimates total aid obligated from 1946 to 2024 at about $298 billion in constant 2024 dollars. The Council on Foreign Relations puts the figure at more than $300 billion. In unadjusted dollars, the total is far lower: CRS data show about $146 billion for 1946 to 2020.

What the current agreement provides

Most of the money now flows under a 10-year Memorandum of Understanding signed in 2016 and covering fiscal years 2019 to 2028. It commits the US, subject to congressional appropriation, to $38 billion over the decade: $33 billion in Foreign Military Financing grants and $5 billion for missile defence. That works out to a baseline of about $3.8 billion a year.

Spread across the US population of roughly 340 million, that baseline is about $11 per person per year, and the cumulative $300 billion is about $880 per person. These per-person figures are theattn calculations from the totals above, not figures published by CRS.

Spending beyond the baseline

Since the Hamas-led attacks of 7 October 2023 and the conflicts that followed in Gaza, Lebanon and Iran, Congress has approved emergency supplemental assistance on top of the annual terms. These packages included $3.5 billion in Foreign Military Financing and a broader $10.6 billion support package that directed more money to missile-defence systems such as Iron Dome and Iron Beam, according to CRS reporting and Senate appropriations documents. Estimates of total post-October 2023 spending vary by what is counted.

How it compares and how it is overseen

According to Council on Foreign Relations data, the next-largest historical recipients, including Egypt, Afghanistan and South Vietnam, each received less than half of Israel’s total. Israel is also a high-income economy with a strong technology sector.

US law bars military aid to foreign security units credibly accused of gross human rights violations, and Israel has agreed to use US-supplied weapons for self-defence. The Council on Foreign Relations notes that some legal scholars say these rules have not been applied to Israel as they have to other countries, and that Congress has never successfully blocked a weapons sale to Israel.

The debate

Supporters of the aid say it serves US strategic interests, strengthens a close ally and a key defence partner, and that a large share of the money is spent with American defence contractors. CRS notes that lawmakers continue to vote in favour of the assistance.

Critics, including some on the political right and left, argue that a wealthy country should not receive billions in US grants while Americans face their own cost-of-living and budget pressures, and that the aid should be reduced or conditioned. CRS also notes calls from some political and ideological groups to reevaluate the long-standing assistance relationship.

The current agreement expires after fiscal year 2028, so the terms of the next one are likely to be a focus of debate in Washington.
