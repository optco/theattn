Teeth That Heal Themselves? Scientists Regrow Real Enamel With a Fluoride-Free Gel

Once enamel is gone, it is gone for good. A protein gel from the University of Nottingham just rebuilt it in the lab, and a first product is already heading for human testing.

8 November 2026, 02:25 · Source: Nature Communications

It is the hardest substance in the human body, and for as long as dentistry has existed, once it is gone, it is gone forever. Now scientists say they have found a way to make it grow back.

A team at the University of Nottingham, led by Dr Abshar Hasan and Professor Alvaro Mata, has created a protein-based gel that coaxes damaged teeth into building new enamel. The gel copies the proteins that guide enamel formation when teeth first develop in infancy, and it contains no fluoride.

Here is how it works. Painted onto a tooth, the gel forms a thin, tough layer that seeps into cracks and holes. That layer then becomes a scaffold, pulling calcium and phosphate straight out of saliva and arranging them into new crystals that lock into the tooth’s existing structure, the same way natural enamel forms. It can even be applied over exposed dentine, the sensitive layer lying beneath enamel.

Then came the stress tests. In lab experiments on human teeth that simulated brushing, chewing and acidic foods, the regenerated enamel behaved just like the healthy stuff. Electron microscope images showed battered, eroded crystals transformed into organised, regrown ones after just two weeks of treatment.

Why it matters

Enamel loss is a global problem. Dental problems linked to enamel degradation affect almost half of the world’s population, and can lead to infections and tooth loss. Today’s fixes, such as fluoride varnishes, can only strengthen what is left, or fill the gap with artificial material. None can rebuild the real thing.

A coating that does could ease sensitivity and repair early damage, and could give fillings and veneers a better surface to bond to.

The reality check

This is not a miracle cure yet. The work was done on teeth in the lab, not in people’s mouths. The new layer is also thin: the gel can restore up to about 10 micrometers of lost enamel, while natural enamel on the biting surface of a tooth can be around 2,000 micrometers thick. How well it holds up in real mouths over years is still unknown.

The researchers say they are moving fast. Hasan and Mata co-founded the start-up Mintech-Bio, which is developing the gel as a dental product called Epinamel. A clinical trial registry entry for the product, aimed at treating early white spot lesions on teeth, lists a completion date of January 2027. Mata has said the team hoped to have a first product out in 2026, though any product will still need regulatory approval.

Still, if it works in people the way it did in the lab, it could mark a turning point: teeth that repair themselves instead of just being patched.

Source: Hasan, A., et al. (2025). Biomimetic supramolecular protein matrix restores structure and properties of human dental enamel. Nature Communications, 16.
