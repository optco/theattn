Single Gene Therapy Injection Restores Hearing in Children and Adults Born Deaf

A one-time inner-ear injection delivering a working OTOF gene improved hearing in all ten patients in a clinical trial, from toddlers to young adults.

21 November 2026, 10:02 · Source: Nature Medicine, Karolinska Institutet

Scientists have restored hearing in children and adults born with a rare form of genetic deafness using a single gene therapy injection into the inner ear.

The treatment targets mutations in the OTOF gene, which makes otoferlin, a protein the ear needs to pass sound signals to the brain. When the gene is faulty, people can be born profoundly deaf or with severe hearing loss.

In the trial, published in Nature Medicine, researchers used a modified, harmless virus to deliver a healthy copy of the gene. It was injected through a membrane at the base of the cochlea known as the round window.

Results within weeks

Ten patients aged between about one and 24 took part at five hospitals in China. Hearing improved in all of them, with much of the gain appearing within the first month. After six months, the average volume at which participants could detect sound improved from about 106 decibels to 52.

Children between the ages of five and eight responded best. One seven-year-old girl recovered almost all of her hearing and was able to hold everyday conversations with her mother four months after treatment. Toddlers and adults also improved, though to a lesser extent.

Safety

No serious adverse reactions were reported during follow-up of six to 12 months. The most common side effect was a drop in neutrophils, a type of white blood cell.

What comes next

The study was small, had no control group, and OTOF-related deafness is rare, so the results do not yet apply to most people with hearing loss. It is also the first time the approach has been tested in teenagers and adults, and longer follow-up is needed.

"OTOF is just the beginning," said Dr Maoli Duan of Karolinska Institutet, one of the study's corresponding authors. Researchers are now working on more common deafness genes, such as GJB2 and TMC1, which are harder to treat but have shown promise in animal studies.
