Cholesterol drug ezetimibe linked to lower dementia risk in study, but trials are needed

Researchers at the University of Arkansas for Medical Sciences found that ezetimibe, a common cholesterol drug, was associated with far lower Alzheimer’s and dementia rates in patient records. The finding is observational and not yet proven in trials.

3 November 2026, 23:52 · Source: Aging Biology, University of Arkansas for Medical Sciences

A widely used, off-patent cholesterol drug may be linked to a much lower risk of Alzheimer’s disease and related dementias, according to researchers at the University of Arkansas for Medical Sciences (UAMS) in Little Rock. The result comes from patient records, not a clinical trial, so it shows an association rather than proof that the drug prevents dementia.

The study, published in the journal Aging Biology in 2024, looked at ezetimibe, sold under the brand name Zetia. The drug lowers cholesterol by blocking its absorption in the gut.

How the drug was found

The team studied two proteins, hexokinase-1 and 14-3-3G, that they found clumped together in brain tissue from people with Alzheimer’s and in heart disease samples. They screened FDA-approved drug structures for ones that might disrupt the contact between the two proteins, and ezetimibe came up as a strong candidate.

In human cell cultures and in roundworm models of dementia-related protein clumping, the researchers reported that ezetimibe disrupted the interaction, reduced aggregation and activated autophagy, the cell’s clean-up process for damaged proteins.

What the patient data showed

The researchers then mined clinical databases, comparing people who took ezetimibe, more than 4,000 of them, with a far larger group who did not. They reported a relative risk of 0.14 for Alzheimer’s and related dementias overall, which is an 86 percent reduction or roughly sevenfold lower risk, with a 95 percent confidence interval of 0.06 to 0.34. In people at higher risk because of heart disease, the relative risk was below 0.12.

Why it is not yet a treatment

Studies that compare patient records can show a link, but they cannot rule out other explanations, such as differences in health, lifestyle or access to care between people who take the drug and people who do not. Randomised clinical trials are needed to test whether ezetimibe protects the brain. Ezetimibe is not approved to treat or prevent Alzheimer’s.

The researchers themselves point to more than one possible explanation. In a UAMS interview, one researcher said lowering cholesterol may also account for part of the effect, as statins have been linked to lower Alzheimer’s risk in other studies.

People who take ezetimibe or any other prescribed medicine should not stop or change it because of this research, and anyone considering a change should talk to their doctor.
